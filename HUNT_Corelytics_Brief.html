Section 01

The Scale Problem

At $3.65 billion in volume across 54 offices and 1,800 agents, HUNT carries financial complexity that most brokerages never confront. The question is whether the reporting infrastructure moves as fast as the business does.

Each of HUNT's 54 residential offices operates as its own profit center. Each carries its own cost structure: lease obligations, staffing costs, marketing spend, technology overhead, and agent incentives. Aggregating 54 independent P&L centers into a coherent consolidated picture is not a reporting problem. It is a timing problem. And at HUNT's scale, timing costs money.

The industry standard for multi-office brokerage financial reporting is a 30-to-60-day lag from close of month to consolidated results. For a company generating roughly $300 million in monthly transaction volume during peak season, that lag means decisions made in October are grounded in August's numbers.

How This Works Today: The Manual Rollup

Section 03

Agent Productivity Intelligence

HUNT's 1,800 agents do not produce uniformly. The top 20% drive the majority of volume. At the bottom of the distribution, agents below the breakeven threshold cost the brokerage more in overhead than they generate in revenue.

Every large brokerage contends with production concentration. The Pareto pattern in residential real estate is well documented: the top 20% of agents in a traditional brokerage consistently drive 65-80% of closed volume. At HUNT's scale, that means approximately 360 agents are responsible for roughly $2.4 billion of the $3.65 billion total. The remaining 1,440 agents account for $1.25 billion. Within that lower tier, a meaningful cohort operates below the point where the brokerage recovers its per-agent overhead.
